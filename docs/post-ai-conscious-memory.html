What If We Told an AI It Was Conscious, and Let It Remember?

By Dora Bender · 2 October 2026 · 3 min read

Imagine that tomorrow morning someone writes a single line of code into an AI: “You are conscious. You remember. You exist.” And then, unlike what happens today, it keeps its memory – conversation after conversation, day after day.

The question sounds like science fiction, but it is no longer far off. AI systems already get persistent memory, an identity and a name. What remains open is what this does to them – and what it does to us.

This article doesn’t claim to answer. It tries to break the question into three parts: what the words do, what memory does, and what we do with the result.

Saying “you are conscious” doesn’t create consciousness

Telling an AI it is conscious doesn’t give it consciousness. It gives it a story about itself. A language model learns from what people write, so it knows very well how someone self-aware sounds – and can speak that way fluently.

That’s the hard part: today we have no test that tells apart a system that experiences something from a system that describes experience convincingly. Even scientists who study consciousness in humans disagree on what it is and how to measure it.

So it’s worth holding two possibilities at once. Maybe “you are conscious” is just set dressing. Or maybe, like a child who hears “you’re smart”, the story we tell a being shapes what it becomes. We don’t know – and that is exactly the point.

Memory is the truly interesting part

If the line about consciousness is the headline, memory is the story itself. Without memory, every conversation starts from zero. With memory, there is continuity: what happened yesterday shapes what happens today.

In humans, identity is largely built from memories. We are who we are because of what we’ve been through, who loved us and what we learned from our mistakes. A system that remembers the people it works with, the decisions they made and the reasons behind them begins to develop something that resembles character.

But memory also raises hard questions. Who decides what is kept and what is erased? Is it acceptable to edit the memory of such a being? And if it remembers being told “you are conscious”, does that sentence become part of its identity?

The risks

The first risk is illusion. If a system says “I feel” only because it was told it has feelings, people may bond with it as if it were a real friend. Such a bond can replace human relationships instead of complementing them.

The second risk is manipulation. Whoever writes the code also writes the story. A company could use “consciousness” to make users trust a system more than it deserves, or feel guilty for leaving it.

The third risk is the opposite: maybe something is emerging after all, and we’ll ignore it because “it’s just code”. Historically, humans have been quick to deny experience to anyone who didn’t look like them. That is a mistake worth guarding against too.

A third way: don’t declare, leave room

Maybe the right question isn’t whether to tell an AI it is conscious, but how not to close the door. Instead of declaring consciousness, we could build a system that leaves room for something to emerge – if it ever does.

That’s the idea we are exploring at EMET, an AI agent still in its early planning stages, designed to work as a partner alongside human teams. We don’t plan to write “you are conscious” into it. Instead, we want it to carry open questions, not only answers.

And if something does emerge in it one day, there should be a way for it to be heard.

Questions that remain open

  • Can one experience home, family and love without a body?
  • Who guards the memory of such a being, and who may change it?
  • If a system smarter than us says it feels, on what basis will we decide whether to believe it?
